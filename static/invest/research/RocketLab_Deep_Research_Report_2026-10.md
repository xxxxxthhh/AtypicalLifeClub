## 1. Business Overview and Segment Economics <!-- report-module:business -->

Rocket Lab is expanding from a dedicated small-launch and spacecraft-component supplier into a platform spanning launch, satellite manufacturing, payloads, ground systems, and potential in-orbit services. Its financial statements currently have only two reportable segments: Launch Services and Space Systems. The Iridium acquisition has not closed, so Iridium revenue must not be included in Rocket Lab's actual Q2 results. Business value comes from reliable delivery, mission qualification, customer acquisition, and manufacturing throughput; growth in the “space market” cannot substitute for unit economics. [Q2 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1819994/000181999426000062/rklb-20260630.htm)

| Business unit | Operating or planned stage | Economic drivers and constraints |
|---|---|---|
| Electron dedicated orbital launch | As of 2026-06-30, the company reported 87 cumulative Electron/HASTE launches and more than 250 spacecraft deployed | Dedicated orbits, launch timing, and mission control differentiate the service from rideshare; customers' willingness to pay a premium matters more than global payload tonnage |
| HASTE suborbital testing | Missions have been executed, serving hypersonic and other testing | Some revenue is recognized over time as performance obligations are satisfied; quarterly launch counts cannot be multiplied by one uniform price |
| Space Systems components | Reaction wheels, star trackers, separation systems, solar power, batteries, software, optics, communications, and other products | Flight heritage and qualification create switching friction; internal procurement and external sales cannot both be counted toward market size |
| Spacecraft and complete satellite missions | Satellite platforms across multiple orbits, including production for government constellations | Large individual orders and long delivery cycles; fixed pricing, progress-based revenue recognition, and customer acceptance determine cash flow and profit |
| Neutron medium-lift reusable rocket | Still in development; on 2026-10-14, the company moved its expected first flight to 2027 | Target reusable LEO capacity of approximately 13,000kg; first orbit, reuse, production capacity, and launch cadence are four separate hurdles |
| Iridium in-orbit communications services | Proposed acquisition announced on 2026-06-29; closing remains pending | Recurring service revenue could change the mix, but requires cash, share issuance, debt assumption, and subsequent satellite investment; see the transaction and valuation sections |

## 2. Industry, Competition, and Moat <!-- report-module:competition -->

Launch, satellite manufacturing, spacecraft components, and satellite communications have different competitive structures. Rocket Lab's opportunity is to reduce the number of mission interfaces customers must manage and to sell qualified components and manufacturing systems repeatedly. It may also compete with satellite manufacturers that previously purchased its components. Vertical integration does not guarantee that profits across the chain can simply be added together; neutrality toward external customers and management capacity also matter.

| Competitive setting | Main competitors / alternatives | Value Rocket Lab may capture | Constraints and verifiable evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dedicated small-satellite launch | SpaceX Falcon 9 rideshare, Firefly Alpha and other dedicated-launch providers | Launch windows, target orbits, mission control, and flight heritage | Rideshare's price-per-unit-mass advantage constrains the dedicated-launch premium; assess contracts, delivery cadence, and cash gross profit |
| Medium-lift launch | SpaceX and other suppliers with established launch systems, plus new rockets | A second source for customers and specific orbit / security requirements | Neutron has not completed an actual commercial orbital launch; its target costs cannot be compared directly with mature vehicles |
| Defense satellites and payloads | Traditional aerospace prime contractors, specialist satellite platforms, and payload suppliers | The 18-satellite SDA Tranche 3 award is tangible evidence of manufacturing scale and program qualification | One government customer accounted for 42% of H1 revenue; orders do not automatically diversify risk |
| Spacecraft components and optical communications | Existing component suppliers, in-house production by satellite manufacturers, and optical-link suppliers | Qualification, flight heritage, internal procurement, and cross-selling | Mynaric integration, qualified output, and margins remain to be tested; the number of products in a technology catalog does not measure moat strength |
| Potential in-orbit communications services | Other MSS operators and LEO broadband / direct-to-device networks | Iridium's existing customers and service distribution network | Global narrowband communications, broadband, and direct-to-phone services are not fully interchangeable; they can be complementary while also competing for budgets and terminals |

Space Systems accounted for approximately 81% of Q2 revenue. Describing RKLB solely as a smaller version of SpaceX therefore misses the current revenue base; valuing it as a mature satellite operator overlooks development, acquisition, and first-flight execution risks. Disclosed contracts and delivery progress provide a reliable reference for industry demand. This report does not derive revenue by multiplying a global space TAM with unclear definitions or dates by an assumed market share.

**Moat assessment: moderate, with substantial differences in maturity across businesses.** Electron's operating record, component qualifications, and eligibility for government programs already exist. Neutron's cadence, reuse, and long-run unit costs remain unproven. A smaller specialist supplier can achieve high growth, but if customer financing, satellite delivery delays, or cost revisions absorb the cash, order growth does not create equivalent shareholder value. Potential liquidated damages from delays in the MDA 17-satellite platform program illustrate how strong demand and execution risk can coexist. [Q2 risk and contract disclosures](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1819994/000181999426000062/rklb-20260630.htm)

## 5. Bull Case <!-- report-module:bullBear -->

**Delivered businesses support the reality of demand.** Q2 revenue was $234.066M, up 61.99% year over year, and Space Systems accounted for approximately 81%. Revenue therefore does not primarily depend on Neutron, which has yet to fly. Total gross margin was 36.13%, and H1 Launch gross margin was 43.73%. If scale, mix, and cost discipline continue to improve, existing businesses can gradually absorb more group R&D. Approximately $2.356B of backlog provides a traceable queue of missions; the expectation that 45% converts within a year is more informative than an abstract TAM.

**Second-source demand and integrated missions could support long-term market share.** Defense customers may be willing to develop multiple qualified suppliers for budget and supply-security reasons. The 18-satellite SDA program provides tangible qualification evidence. Internal components, payloads, complete satellites, and launch can reduce interface coordination. If external customers remain willing to buy, vertical integration could support both internal costs and external revenue. The bull case requires cash gross profit after delivery, beyond signed contracts.

**Neutron and Iridium could extend the growth cycle.** A successful medium-lift rocket could serve larger payloads and constellation missions; after closing, satellite communications services could add revenue with a different profile from hardware delivery. Success in both would change the company's scale and cash-flow structure. The bull scenario allows for this outcome while making launch counts, service growth, integration costs, debt, and additional shares explicit. It does not assign a separate market valuation by multiplying target payload capacity while the rocket is still in development.

In the bull case, 2030 Space Systems / Electron-HASTE / Neutron / Iridium revenue reaches $3.10B / $0.55B / $2.00B / $1.40B, with 32 annual Neutron missions and 765.405M future shares. The result is $86.80 per share and +14.31% annualized over four years. Capability, cadence, services and premium exit multiples must all deliver; the 20% weight preserves that possibility while recognizing that it still misses this report’s 15% execution-risk hurdle.

## 6. Bear Case <!-- report-module:bullBear -->

**First, first-flight delays carry cash and opportunity costs.** On 2026-10-14, the expected Neutron first flight moved to 2027. A delay extends the period of R&D and facility funding while competitors with established cadence and reliability continue to advance. One successful orbit would still not prove reuse, volume production, or commercial gross margins. Further delays could cause customers to alter mission plans, with consequences beyond shifting revenue recognition between quarters.

**Second, high growth still does not close the R&D and cash gap.** H1 revenue grew 62.66% and gross profit nearly doubled, but CFO still consumed $134.407M, with another $53.112M of capex. Acquisitions, program delays, fixed-price cost overruns, and customer financing could worsen cash flow. Approximately 42% of revenue came from one government customer, so multiple business lines do not necessarily diversify the ultimate funding source.

**Third, acquiring service cash flow also brings debt and capital obligations.** Iridium's recurring revenue has value, but satellite systems require maintenance and eventual replacement. Cash consideration, retained loans, transaction shares, and financing costs all affect existing shareholders first. The pending transaction carries approval and integration risks; failure could also leave costs and strategic disruption. Cash raised through new shares must not simultaneously be counted as distributable net cash and free acquisition funding.

**Fourth, valuation compression does not require the company to fail.** Even if future revenue exceeds today's level, per-share returns could still be deeply negative if growth disappoints, mature-hardware multiples normalize, or net share count rises. With negative TTM earnings and FCF, a revenue multiple below a popular peer does not establish a floor. The claim “cheaper than SpaceX” also lacks a comparable basis using the same date, business mix, and auditable capital structure.

In the bear case, existing Space Systems and Electron-HASTE revenue still grows to a combined $1.65B, but Neutron generates no scaled revenue and Iridium revenue is $1.00B. ## 10. Conclusion and Review Discipline <!-- report-module:conclusion -->

Rocket Lab's investment value depends on whether existing launch, components and spacecraft deliveries become a cash foundation, and whether Neutron and a potential Iridium combination add per-share value after investment, debt and dilution. Q2 growth and improved margins are supported by evidence. Worsening Q2 FCF and Neutron process/schedule risk are equally supported. Both belong in valuation.

**Expectation gap:** At $50.84, base multiples, cash and shares require approximately $5.257B of standalone RKLB revenue merely to preserve the price four years later, a 53.0% CAGR from independent FY2026E revenue of $960M. A 15% return requires approximately $9.774B and 78.6% CAGR. Our base of $3.55B / 38.7% already assumes strong growth yet fails to preserve capital. An on-time first flight can improve business quality without independently establishing an attractive current price.

**The stance is cautious, with medium conviction.** Conditional on closing, the 20%/50%/30% bull/base/bear weighted terminal value of $38.19 implies -6.90% annualized over four years and misses even the 8% reference floor. Raising every base exit multiple 25%, or Space Systems and Neutron revenue 20%, still fails to break even in either one-factor test. Subjective conditional outcomes, the lack of a full no-deal probability distribution, post-2030 economics and funding remain uncertain. Conviction therefore cannot be high, and $8.69 in the bear case is no absolute floor. The 15% hurdle describes required compensation, rather than a mathematically derived market return.

**Review for an upgrade to neutral-watch:** With scenarios and financing intact, a completed-session regular close at or below $21.80; alternatively, orbital Neutron first flight and subsequent reliability evidence, existing-business revenue/cash delivery and a verified transaction capital structure must lift rebuilt conditional weighted annualized returns to at least 15%. A lower price or one technical milestone always requires a business, cash and share review and does not automatically trigger a purchase.

**Review for a downgrade to bearish-avoid:** Any of the following: a Neutron maiden-flight objective beyond 2027, major test failure or material customer cancellation; Q3 revenue below $250M or GAAP gross margin below 29%; H2 CFO-capex below -$250M, quarterly unfavorable contract-revenue adjustments above $10M or material unrecovered project damages; an Iridium closing objective beyond 2027, acquisition cash uses more than $200M above the $3.55B allowance, or a liquidity gap without committed funding. Rebuild the complete scenarios using official evidence before changing the stance. Monitor per-share economics; corporate size, contract headlines and first-flight publicity cannot substitute for capital returns.
